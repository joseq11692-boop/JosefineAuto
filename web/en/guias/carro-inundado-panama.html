How to spot a flood-damaged car in Panama

Five five-minute checks so you don't buy a car that has been underwater, plus what to do if it was imported from an auction.

It rains hard in Panama for much of the year, and many used cars arrive imported from auctions in the United States. Some of them were underwater. On the outside they can look spotless. On the inside, the moisture keeps working: it corrodes connectors, damages electronic modules and shows up months later as electrical faults that are hard to track down.

These are the checks we do on every car before buying it. You don't need tools: just a flashlight and five minutes.

1. Lift the carpet

Look for dried mud, sand, water stains or moisture under the floor and trunk carpets. A car that got wet through an open window has a damp carpet; a flooded one usually has sediment underneath.

2. Check the seat rails and the screws

With the flashlight, check the metal seat rails and the screws under the dashboard. Rust on parts that should never get wet is a clear sign.

3. Check the electrical connectors

There are wiring connectors under the seats. If they have green or white dust (corrosion), the water got that far.

4. Look for water marks in the headlights and dashboard

A line of condensation or sediment inside the headlights, or stains in the instrument cluster, give away standing water.

5. Trust your nose

A damp or moldy smell doesn't go away with air freshener. If the car smells strongly of perfume, be suspicious.

If the car is imported

Ask for the chassis number (VIN) and check its history on services like Carfax or AutoCheck. If it shows up as flood, salvage (total loss) or rebuilt, better not.

A flooded car can look perfect the day you buy it. Cheap ends up expensive.
